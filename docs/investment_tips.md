# Real Estate Investment Tips: Evaluating Rental Property Like an Investor

*Educational content for PropMind. Figures are illustrative examples, not predictions or investment advice. Taxes and landlord rules vary, so consult a tax professional and local landlord-tenant rules before investing.*

## 1. Start With the Goal

Investors usually pursue one of three goals: monthly cash flow, long-term appreciation, or a mix of both with the help of mortgage paydown. Cash-flow markets often have lower prices relative to rent but slower growth. Appreciation markets can reward patient owners but may produce thin or negative monthly income. Decide which matters most, how long you can hold, and how much surprise expense you can absorb before choosing a property.

## 2. The Core Metrics

- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** annual rent minus vacancy and operating expenses, before mortgage payments.
- **Capitalization rate (cap rate):** NOI divided by purchase price. It compares properties independent of financing.
- **Cash-on-cash return:** annual cash flow after the mortgage divided by the cash you actually invested (down payment, closing costs and repairs).
- **Gross rent multiplier (GRM):** price divided by annual gross rent. Lower is generally better, but it ignores expenses.
- **Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR):** NOI divided by annual mortgage payments. Lenders for investment loans often want at least 1.2.

Rules of thumb such as the "1% rule" (monthly rent at least 1% of price) or the "50% rule" (expenses average about half of rent, excluding the mortgage) are quick filters, not substitutes for a real budget.

## 3. A Worked Example

Suppose you buy a house for $200,000 that rents for $1,700 per month.

- Gross annual rent: $20,400. After a 6% vacancy allowance: about $19,176.
- Operating expenses: property tax $3,200, insurance $1,100, maintenance (1% of price) $2,000, property management (8%) about $1,534, capital expenditure reserve $1,200. Total about $9,034.
- NOI is about $10,142, so the cap rate is about 5.07%.

With a 20% down payment ($40,000) plus about $4,000 in closing costs, you invest $44,000 and borrow $160,000. At a 6.5% fixed rate over 30 years the mortgage payment is about $1,011 per month, or roughly $12,135 per year. Cash flow is NOI minus debt service, which is about negative $1,990 per year, a cash-on-cash return near negative 4.5%.

The lesson: when the cap rate is lower than the mortgage rate, borrowing reduces cash flow ("negative leverage"). Such a deal works only if you expect strong appreciation, rent growth, or you plan to live in one unit. Run your own numbers before trusting a listing's promises.

## 4. Underwrite Conservatively

Use realistic assumptions: vacancy of 5% to 8% (higher in student or seasonal markets), maintenance of 1% to 2% of value per year for older homes, a separate capital expenditure reserve for roof, HVAC and water heater, and 8% to 10% for professional management even if you plan to self-manage, because your time has value. Add 3% to 5% for annual rent changes only if the market supports it. Stress-test the deal: if rent falls 10% and the rate on a variable loan rises 2 points, can you still cover costs?

## 5. Location Signals That Matter

Strong rental locations show diverse employers, rising population, low vacancy, and limited new supply. In a university town like Ames, rental demand near campus follows the academic calendar, leases often start in August, and turnover and vacancy risk concentrate in summer. Properties near employers and schools usually rent faster than houses at the edge of town. Check neighborhood trends such as price per square foot and median price in the PropMind Insights page, look at crime and flood risk, and drive the area at different times of day.

## 6. Quality, Age and Condition

In the Ames data, overall build quality and living area are the strongest drivers of sale price. Older homes can have lower purchase prices but higher repair costs and rent ceilings limited by condition. Before buying, price out the roof, foundation, plumbing, electrical and HVAC. A house that needs $30,000 of unseen repairs is not cheap.

## 7. Financing an Investment Property

Lenders treat investment loans as higher risk: expect a larger down payment (often 15% to 25%), slightly higher rates, and stricter reserves. Owner-occupant strategies, such as buying a duplex or a house with a rentable basement and living in one unit, may qualify for lower down payments and rates. This approach, known as house hacking, lowers your own housing cost while you learn to be a landlord.

## 8. Taxes and Depreciation

Rental income is taxable, but you can usually deduct mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, repairs and management costs. Residential rental buildings are depreciated over 27.5 years in the United States, which can reduce taxable income. Selling can trigger capital gains tax and depreciation recapture. A tax professional can help you structure ownership (personal name, LLC or other) and plan an exit.

## 9. Risk Checklist

1. Vacancy longer than planned and slow tenant screening.
2. Large repairs not covered by insurance.
3. Rising interest rates on variable-rate loans or refinancing risk.
4. Concentration: all your wealth in one property or one neighborhood.
5. Legal risk from lease mistakes, fair-housing violations or unsafe conditions.
6. Liquidity: houses take weeks or months to sell and cost 6% to 10% to sell.

Maintain landlord insurance, an emergency reserve of at least six months of expenses, and a written screening policy applied equally to every applicant.

## 10. Quick Decision Framework

Buy only when the numbers work with conservative inputs, the property condition is verified by an inspection, the neighborhood has stable demand, and you could hold through a bad year. Compare the price with a data-driven estimate: a property priced well below its predicted value in a desirable area is worth a closer look, while one priced far above the estimate needs a clear justification such as unique features.
