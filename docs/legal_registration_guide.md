# Registry and Legal Process for Buying a Home in Iowa

*Educational summary for PropMind users, centered on Iowa where the Ames housing data comes from. Rules and fees change and vary by county. This is not legal advice; confirm details with a licensed Iowa real estate attorney, your title company and the county recorder before signing anything.*

## 1. Who Is Involved

A typical purchase involves the buyer and seller, real estate agents or brokers licensed by the Iowa Real Estate Commission, a lender, an attorney (strongly recommended in Iowa, where attorneys commonly prepare and examine title documents), a title company or closing agent, an inspector, an appraiser, and the County Recorder who records the deed. Ames is in Story County, so a deed for an Ames property is recorded with the Story County Recorder.

## 2. The Offer to Buy (Purchase Agreement)

Iowa transactions commonly use the standard Offer to Buy Real Estate form maintained by the Iowa State Bar Association, often alongside the Iowa Realtors forms. The agreement is a binding contract once accepted in writing. It normally states the legal description of the property, price, earnest money, financing terms, possession date, what stays with the house (appliances, window coverings), inspection rights, and the deadline for each contingency. Read every deadline carefully, because missing a contingency date can mean losing the right to cancel without penalty.

## 3. Earnest Money and Escrow

The buyer deposits earnest money, usually into a real estate broker's trust account or with a title or closing agent. It is applied to the purchase price at closing. If the buyer withdraws for a reason allowed by the contract, such as a failed inspection contingency, the deposit is normally returned; if the buyer defaults without a contractual reason, the seller may claim it. Disputes should be resolved in writing and, when needed, with an attorney.

## 4. Seller Disclosure Documents

Iowa law (Iowa Code chapter 558A) generally requires sellers of residential property with one to four dwelling units to give buyers a written disclosure statement covering known conditions such as roof and basement problems, water or sewer issues, insect damage, hazardous materials and underground tanks. Certain transfers are exempt, for example some estate and bank sales. Sellers also provide the federal lead-based paint disclosure for homes built before 1978, and buyers have a period to test for lead. Radon is widespread in Iowa, so request or order a radon test even where a disclosure says nothing.

A disclosure is not a warranty and does not replace a professional inspection. Keep copies of all signed disclosures.

## 5. Title Examination and Title Insurance

Clear title means the seller can legally transfer ownership without competing claims. In Iowa the title is usually examined from an abstract of title or from records, and an attorney issues a title opinion that lists defects such as unpaid liens, unreleased mortgages, judgments, easements and boundary issues. Defects must be cleared before closing.

Title insurance protects against hidden defects that were not discovered, such as forged documents or unknown heirs. Iowa also has a state-run program, Iowa Title Guaranty, administered through the Iowa Finance Authority, which many buyers use as an alternative to private title insurance. Lenders almost always require a lender's policy; an owner's policy is a separate purchase worth considering.

## 6. Survey, Zoning and Flood Risk

Review a survey or plat to confirm boundaries, fences and easements. Check zoning with the city planning office to confirm the allowed use, additions, accessory dwellings and rental limits. Ask whether the home lies in a FEMA flood zone, because flood insurance is separate from standard homeowners insurance and is required by lenders in high-risk areas. A parcel near a creek or river can be at risk even if the house itself has never flooded.

## 7. Deeds and Transfer Documents

The seller conveys ownership by a deed, most often a **warranty deed**, in which the seller promises good title and defends against claims. A **quit claim deed** transfers only whatever interest the grantor has, with no warranty, and is common between family members or to fix paperwork errors. At closing you should also expect a declaration of value form filed with the transfer, a closing statement or Closing Disclosure, the mortgage and note, and any affidavits the title examiner requires. If the property is held by a married seller, spousal signature or waiver rules may apply.

## 8. Taxes and Fees at Closing

- **Real estate transfer tax:** Iowa charges a transfer tax calculated per $500 of value, with the first $500 exempt. Confirm the current rate with the county recorder; it is customarily paid by the seller.
- **Recording fees:** paid to the county recorder for recording the deed and mortgage.
- **Property taxes:** in Iowa, property taxes are billed in two installments, due by the end of September and the end of March, and are paid in arrears. At closing the taxes are prorated, so the seller pays for the days they owned the house. Ask whether any homestead credit applies after you move in, and file for it by the deadline.
- **Other costs:** lender fees, title examination or insurance, appraisal, inspection, prepaid insurance and escrow deposits.

## 9. Closing and Recording

At closing, funds are disbursed, documents are signed, and the deed is delivered. The deed and the mortgage must then be recorded at the County Recorder's office so that the public record shows you as the owner. Recording protects you against later claims, so make sure it is completed and keep recorded copies. Update the homeowners insurance, utilities and property tax mailing address immediately.

## 10. Red Flags to Raise With Your Attorney

1. The seller is not the person on the record title, or the name differs between documents.
2. Unreleased liens, unpaid contractor claims or tax delinquencies.
3. Missing permits for additions, finished basements or electrical work.
4. Seller refuses to give the standard disclosure or allow an inspection.
5. Pressure to wire money outside the title company or to change closing instructions at the last minute.
6. A property in an estate, divorce, or trust sale where authority to sell has not been confirmed.

## 11. Document Checklist

Signed offer to buy, earnest money receipt, seller disclosure statement, lead-based paint disclosure if built before 1978, inspection reports, title opinion or title insurance commitment, survey or plat, loan documents, Closing Disclosure, deed, declaration of value, proof of insurance, and recorded deed copy after closing.
