# Property Buying Guide: A Step-by-Step Handbook for Home Buyers

*Educational material prepared for PropMind. It explains the general home-buying process in the United States and is not financial, legal or tax advice.*

## 1. Know Your Numbers First

Before looking at a single listing, work out what you can genuinely afford. Lenders commonly use two guidelines. The front-end ratio says housing costs (principal, interest, property tax, insurance and any HOA dues) should stay under about 28% of gross monthly income. The back-end ratio, also called debt-to-income or DTI, says all monthly debt payments including the new mortgage should stay under about 36% of gross income. Many lenders approve up to 43% or more, but a loan you are allowed to take is not always a loan you can live with comfortably.

Budget for more than the sticker price. Closing costs usually run 2% to 5% of the purchase price (lender fees, title work, appraisal, prepaid taxes and insurance). After moving in, plan roughly 1% of the home's value per year for maintenance and repairs, more for older houses. Keep an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses that is separate from your down payment.

## 2. Down Payment and Loan Types

A 20% down payment avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI) on a conventional loan, but it is not required. Common options include:

- **Conventional loans:** often 3% to 5% down for qualified first-time buyers; PMI applies below 20% down and can be removed once you reach 20% equity (it must end automatically at 78% of the original value if payments are current).
- **FHA loans:** backed by the Federal Housing Administration, as little as 3.5% down with a credit score of 580 or higher, with upfront and annual mortgage insurance premiums.
- **VA loans:** for eligible veterans and service members, often with no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance.
- **USDA loans:** for eligible rural and suburban areas and income limits, sometimes with no down payment.

Compare the annual percentage rate (APR), not only the interest rate, because APR includes many lender fees. A 30-year fixed loan gives the lowest payment and a predictable budget; a 15-year loan costs less interest overall but needs a much higher monthly payment.

## 3. Get Pre-Approved, Not Just Pre-Qualified

Pre-qualification is a quick estimate based on information you state yourself. Pre-approval is stronger: the lender verifies income, assets and credit and issues a letter stating how much it is willing to lend. Sellers take offers with a pre-approval letter far more seriously. Avoid opening new credit cards, financing a car or changing jobs between pre-approval and closing, because lenders re-check your finances before funding.

## 4. Separate Needs From Wants

Write two lists. Needs are the things you cannot compromise on: a maximum price, a minimum number of bedrooms, a commute limit, a school district, accessibility. Wants are what you would love but can live without: a finished basement, granite counters, a large yard. The location and the structure cannot be changed later, while finishes can. Buyers who overpay for cosmetic upgrades often regret not paying for a better location or a newer roof.

## 5. Searching and Evaluating Homes

Study comparable sales ("comps") from the last three to six months: similar size, age and condition within a short distance. Compare price per square foot, but do so carefully, because a small house with a large lot or a renovated kitchen can distort the number. Look at days on market, price reductions and the ratio of sale price to list price to understand how competitive the area is. A data-driven estimate, such as the PropMind price prediction, is a useful second opinion beside an agent's comparative market analysis.

When visiting a house, check water stains on ceilings, doors and windows that stick, uneven floors, the age of the roof, furnace, water heater and electrical panel, and how the lot drains after rain.

## 6. Making an Offer

An offer states the price, earnest money deposit (commonly 1% to 3% of the price), closing date, and contingencies. The main contingencies protect you: **inspection** (you can renegotiate or walk away over defects), **financing** (you can exit if the loan falls through) and **appraisal** (if the home appraises below the price, you can renegotiate or cancel). Waiving contingencies makes an offer stronger but exposes you to real financial risk, so do it only with a clear understanding of the downside.

If several buyers are competing, an escalation clause, a larger earnest deposit or a flexible closing date can help without simply raising the price. Never bid above the amount you can sustain after taxes, insurance and repairs.

## 7. Inspection and Appraisal

Hire an independent licensed inspector, not one recommended only by the seller's side. A general inspection covers structure, roof, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, and visible moisture. Ask separately about radon testing (important in the Midwest), termites and wood-destroying insects, sewer line scope for older homes, and lead paint or asbestos for houses built before 1978 and 1980 respectively. Use the report to negotiate repairs, a price reduction or seller credits.

The lender orders an appraisal to confirm the home is worth the loan amount. If the appraisal comes in low you can ask the seller to reduce the price, pay the gap in cash, or cancel under the appraisal contingency.

## 8. Closing

About three business days before closing you must receive the Closing Disclosure, which lists final loan terms and costs. Compare it to your Loan Estimate and question anything that changed. Do a final walkthrough within 24 hours of closing to confirm agreed repairs were completed and nothing was damaged. At closing you sign the loan documents and deed paperwork, pay the remaining funds by wire or cashier's check, and receive the keys. Always confirm wire instructions by phone using a number you already trust, because wire fraud targeting buyers is common.

## 9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Stretching to the maximum approved loan instead of a comfortable payment.
2. Skipping the inspection or the appraisal to win a bidding war.
3. Forgetting property tax, insurance, utilities and maintenance when comparing homes.
4. Falling in love with a house and ignoring comparable sales.
5. Taking on new debt before closing.
6. Choosing a lender only by interest rate without comparing fees.

## 10. Buyer Checklist

- Review credit reports and fix errors three to six months before shopping.
- Set a budget using the 28/36 guideline and add a cushion.
- Obtain a pre-approval letter and compare at least three lenders.
- Shortlist neighborhoods and study recent comparable sales.
- Make offers with protective contingencies and a realistic earnest deposit.
- Complete inspection, appraisal and title review.
- Review the Closing Disclosure, do the final walkthrough, and verify wire instructions.
